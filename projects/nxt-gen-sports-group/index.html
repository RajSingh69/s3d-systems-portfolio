The project

NXT Gen is not just one website job. It grew into several connected pieces of work around the same sports organisation: the public website, the keynote speaking side, a private player-management platform and social media support.

Part one: the website

I rebuilt the public-facing website, including the homepage, services, speaker information and equality, diversity and inclusion content. The design has a strong, dark visual style that fits the organisation. It provides the public introduction to what NXT Gen does and the areas it works in.

Part two: keynote speaking

The speaking section includes speaker profiles and booking pages, such as those for Sunny Singh Gill and Roopa Vyas. This needed a different kind of content from the main services pages: the individual speaker, their experience and how someone can enquire about a booking.

Part three: player management In Development

The private platform is the most software-heavy part of the NXT Gen work. It includes a dashboard for athletes, teams and organisations, with athlete profiles covering areas such as wellbeing, education, nutrition and goals. It works end to end but is still early in development, and is shown here with test data only.

Part four: social media

I've also worked on content and the organisation's social presence. This is a useful example of how S3D can support both the technical side of a project and the material people actually see.

Technical deep dive

Under the hood

Website

The public site is hand-built HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with responsive navigation.

Athlete platform

The private platform runs on Firebase, with separate areas for athletes, teams, organisations, development, reviews, safeguarding, reports, users and settings.

My experience

What makes NXT Gen interesting to me is that it didn't stay in one box. A website led to speaker pages, then a platform, then ongoing content work. I liked seeing how one relationship could develop into different kinds of work, each needing a different approach. The athlete platform in particular brought me back to building structured software rather than just pages.

What I'd take forward

I would keep the four workstreams clearly separated in terms of scope and status, even when they sit together. That makes it easier to explain what has launched, what is still being developed and what I'm continuing to support.
